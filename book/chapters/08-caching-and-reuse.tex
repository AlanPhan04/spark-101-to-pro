\chapter{Caching and Reuse}
\label{ch:caching}

\begin{chapterintro}
Caching keeps a result Spark would otherwise recompute --- but the wrong cache
wastes storage memory and starves execution, and a cache used once is pure
overhead. On the lab, caching one week of \texttt{orders} for two aggregations
was slower than not caching at all; it needed about ten reads to break even.
Spark also reuses work you did not ask it to: an identical exchange computed once
and read twice, which cut a join from 10.0 to 7.5 seconds. This chapter covers
when caching pays, which storage level to pick against the GC budget from
\Cref{ch:execmem}, how to see reuse in a plan, and what happens when execution
and storage memory finally meet at runtime. Every number is a capture from the
lab of \Cref{sec:refenv-lab}.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{When caching pays}
\label{sec:caching-when}

A cached DataFrame pays for itself only when two things are both true: it is read
enough times to recover the cost of building the cache, and it fits comfortably
inside the executor's storage memory (\Cref{sec:mem-model}) without pushing out
other useful cached data. Caching something read exactly once adds the cost of
writing it into storage memory (and, if it does not fit, spilling it to disk) for
a saving that never arrives. The classic mistake is caching immediately before a
single \texttt{write()}.

How many reads is enough is a number you can measure rather than guess. The lab
cached one week of \texttt{orders} (6.8 million rows, 563~MB cached) and ran the
same aggregation $N$ times, with and without the cache.
\Cref{tab:caching-lab-breakeven} and \Cref{fig:cache-breakeven} show the result.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Reads} $N$ & \textbf{No cache} & \textbf{Build cache} & \textbf{$N$ cached reads} & \textbf{Cache total} \\
\midrule
1 & 2.77~s & 4.94~s & 1.66~s & 6.60~s \\
2 & 3.15~s & 4.13~s & 2.56~s & 6.69~s \\

\end{tabular}
\caption{Wall-clock seconds for $N$ aggregations over one week of \texttt{orders},

\label{tab:caching-lab-breakeven}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{cache-breakeven.pdf}
  \caption{Total time for $N$ aggregations over the same week, with and without
  \texttt{cache()}. The shaded gap is time the cache costs; the lines converge
  near ten reads because each cached read still scans 563~MB of cached batches
  against 58~MB of Parquet columns.}
  \label{fig:cache-breakeven}
\end{figure}

The arithmetic generalizes. If building the cache costs $M$ seconds, an uncached
read costs $R$, and a cached read costs $C$, caching pays after $M / (R - C)$
reads: here $4.2 / (1.44 - 1.04) \approx 10$. The break-even moves with the
source. A Parquet scan from local disk is cheap, so the cache needs many reads;
a scan of object storage, or an expensive upstream join, makes $R$ large and the
break-even small. Measure $R$ for your source before deciding.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL,caption={Reused twice: worth caching only if the reads are costly},captionpos=b,label=lst:caching-reused]
-- filtered_orders is read by two different aggregations
filtered_orders.cache()
filtered_orders.count()                 -- materializes the cache
result1 = filtered_orders.join(dim_product, "product_id")
result2 = filtered_orders.groupBy("product_id").sum("order_total")
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Lazy \texttt{cache()} versus eager \texttt{CACHE TABLE}}
\label{sec:caching-lazy}

\texttt{df.cache()} does not cache anything by itself --- it only marks the
DataFrame for caching the next time an action runs it, consistent with
transformations being lazy everywhere else (\Cref{sec:shape-jobs}). The lab's
Storage tab shows it: \Cref{tab:caching-lab-lazy} lists what was resident after
each step.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}P{6.2cm}rr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Step} & \textbf{Cached partitions} & \textbf{In memory} \\
\midrule
\texttt{df.cache()}, no action yet & none & 0 \\
\texttt{df.count()} & 7 of 7 & 563.3~MB \\
\texttt{CACHE LAZY TABLE wk\_lazy AS \ldots} & none & 0 \\
first query on \texttt{wk\_lazy} & 7 of 7 & 563.3~MB \\
\texttt{CACHE TABLE wk\_eager AS \ldots} & 7 of 7 & 563.3~MB \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{What the Storage tab holds after each step. Only \texttt{CACHE TABLE}
caches before any query reads it.}
\label{tab:caching-lab-lazy}
\end{table}

Without a materializing action, the first real use of the DataFrame both computes
it \emph{and} populates the cache as a side effect, which works but hands you no
control over when the cost is paid; a cheap action such as \texttt{count()} right
after \texttt{cache()} pays it on your own terms. SQL's \texttt{CACHE TABLE} is
the one place this default flips: it is \emph{eager}, executing immediately.
Use \texttt{CACHE LAZY TABLE} for the lazy behavior \texttt{df.cache()} gives you.

\section{Storage levels and the GC trade-off}
\label{sec:caching-storage-levels}

The default storage level for a DataFrame, \conf{MEMORY_AND_DISK}, keeps
cached blocks in storage memory and spills to disk only when they do not fit.
What the blocks \emph{are} matters for the arithmetic. Spark SQL caches a
DataFrame as compressed columnar batches (\conf{spark.sql.inMemoryColumnarStorage.compressed},
default \texttt{true}), not as millions of \texttt{Row} objects, so the
per-object overhead of \Cref{sec:mem-heap} applies to the RDD API, not here. The
lab measured the same 6.8 million rows at three levels (\Cref{tab:caching-lab-levels}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}P{6.0cm}rr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{How it was cached} & \textbf{In memory} & \textbf{On disk} \\
\midrule
\texttt{df.cache()} (Scala default, deserialized) & 563.3~MB & 0 \\
\conf{MEMORY_AND_DISK_SER} (serialized) & 410.5~MB & 0 \\
\texttt{DISK\_ONLY} & 0 & 410.5~MB \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{One week of \texttt{orders} at three storage levels. Serializing saved
27\%, not the 2--3$\times$ that holds for caches of JVM objects.}
\label{tab:caching-lab-levels}
\end{table}

So the deserialized default costs about a quarter more memory than serialized,
and the serialized form must be decoded again on every read. Whether that trade
is worth it depends on GC: a large, long-lived cache is the workload that pushes
a collector toward its expensive full-heap path (\Cref{sec:mem-gc}). Two levels
exist to trade that away:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \conf{MEMORY_AND_DISK_SER} keeps the cache serialized, one contiguous
        block per partition, at the cost of decoding rows back out on every read.
  \item \texttt{OFF\_HEAP} (with \conf{spark.memory.offHeap.enabled}) puts the
        cache outside the JVM heap, the pool from \Cref{sec:mem-model}: zero GC
        overhead for that data, at the cost of the off-heap capacity it consumes.
        The lab cached 30 million rows (three columns) at \texttt{OFF\_HEAP} with a 1~GB
off-heap pool: Spark's memory manager showed 1,063~MB of storage in the off-heap pool and
0.1~MB on-heap, and the pool being full, 310~MB of the cache went to disk (the level
reported was \emph{disk, memory, offheap}). Off-heap caching therefore keeps the bytes
out of the heap exactly as claimed, and a pool smaller than the data silently spills
to disk rather than failing.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{DISK\_ONLY} is rarely the right answer: reading a cache from disk is close
enough to re-reading the source that the cache buys little.

\begin{warning}
PySpark's \conf{StorageLevel.MEMORY_AND_DISK} is the \emph{serialized} level
(its \texttt{deserialized} flag is false), while Scala's \texttt{df.cache()} uses
the deserialized one. On the lab the two differ by 153~MB for the same rows,
which is the 27\% of \Cref{tab:caching-lab-levels}. Check the Storage tab's
level column rather than trusting the constant's name.
\end{warning}

Switch away from the deserialized default when GC time is 5--10\% or more of
task time for a job that caches heavily (\Cref{sec:reading-metrics}). Below that,
the decoding cost of a serialized level is not worth paying.

\section{How execution and storage memory borrow at runtime}
\label{sec:caching-borrowing}

\Cref{sec:mem-model} described unified memory's split between execution and
storage as a soft boundary set by \conf{spark.memory.storageFraction}; here is
what that means while a job actually runs. Either side may use the other's
\emph{idle} space: a job with nothing cached lets execution use the whole unified
pool for shuffles and joins, and a job running no wide operators lets storage use
all of it for cached blocks.

The boundary only matters once both sides want space at the same time. Suppose
storage's own half of a 4~GB unified pool is 2~GB. A pipeline caches
\conf{filtered_orders} and the cache grows to 3~GB: 2~GB in storage's own half
plus 1~GB borrowed from execution's idle half. A join then starts and its sort
buffers need 1.5~GB. Execution is entitled to reclaim its space, so Spark evicts
blocks from the borrowed 1~GB (LRU order); if the join later needs more than its
own half, it spills to disk rather than blocking. Run the scenario in reverse ---
the join first, then the cache --- and storage cannot evict a word of the join's
live hash table; it is capped at whatever its guaranteed half has free until the
join releases the borrowed space. The two directions are not symmetric:

\begin{itemize}
  \item if execution needs memory that storage is borrowing, Spark evicts cached
        blocks (LRU) to reclaim it, down to storage's own guaranteed half;
  \item if storage needs memory that execution is borrowing, Spark cannot evict a
        running task's hash table or sort buffer; storage waits until the task
        finishes.
\end{itemize}

Execution always wins a live contest, because failing to complete a running
operator is worse than evicting a cache entry and recomputing it later
(\Cref{fig:memory-borrowing-eviction}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{memory-borrowing-eviction.pdf}
  \caption{Borrowing is symmetric; reclaiming is not. Storage can spread into
  idle execution space, but the moment a join or sort needs that space back,
  execution evicts the borrower's cached blocks to reclaim it --- storage has no
  equivalent power over a running task.}
  \label{fig:memory-borrowing-eviction}
\end{figure}

The lab reproduced the eviction. With a 4~GB heap (about 2.3~GB of unified
memory) the whole month of \texttt{orders} was cached at
\conf{MEMORY_AND_DISK}: 2253~MB in memory and 176~MB on disk, all 31 of 31
partitions resident. A join to \texttt{dim\_customer} then ran in the same
application and needed execution memory. Afterwards the cache still held 31 of 31
partitions, but 1931~MB in memory and 410~MB on disk: 322~MB of blocks had moved
from memory to disk to make room, arriving as 235~MB because the disk copy is
serialized (\Cref{fig:cache-eviction-measured}). Because
the level includes disk, nothing was lost; with \texttt{MEMORY\_ONLY} those blocks
would have been dropped and recomputed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{cache-eviction-measured.pdf}
  \caption{A cached month of \texttt{orders} before and after a join ran. All
  partitions stay cached, but 235~MB more of them sit on disk once execution
  claimed the memory it needed.}
  \label{fig:cache-eviction-measured}
\end{figure}

\section{Exchange and subquery reuse}
\label{sec:caching-reuse}

Caching is a decision you make; Spark also reuses identical work on its own when
the physical plan contains the same subtree twice. If two operators need data
shuffled the same way --- same partitioning, same partition count, same
aggregate --- the planner reuses one exchange for both
(\texttt{ReuseExchange}, \Cref{sec:c-exchange}); an identical scalar subquery
appearing more than once is deduplicated the same way (\texttt{ReuseSubquery}).
Both are visible in the physical plan and need no caching from you.
\Cref{fig:reuse-exchange} and \Cref{tab:caching-lab-reuse} measure the effect on a
self-join of a per-product aggregate.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{reuse-exchange.pdf}
  \caption{The same self-join of an aggregate planned two ways. With reuse, the
  scan and exchange run once and the join reads the result twice
  (\texttt{ReusedExchange}); without it, the scan and the shuffle write run twice.}
  \label{fig:reuse-exchange}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}P{5.2cm}rrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Plan} & \textbf{Scan stages} & \textbf{\texttt{ReusedExchange}} & \textbf{Wall clock} \\
\midrule
same aggregate twice, reuse on & 1 (173~MB) & 1 & 7.5~s \\
same aggregate twice, \conf{spark.sql.exchange.reuse}=false & 2 (346~MB) & 0 & 10.0~s \\

\end{tabular}
\caption{Reuse on the lab. Disabling it doubles the scan and costs about a third

and key differ in their aggregate function, so the exchanges differ and nothing
is reused.}
\label{tab:caching-lab-reuse}
\end{table}

Reuse is fragile in exactly the way \Cref{sec:shuffle-unnecessary} warned about:
a partition count, column set, or aggregate function that differs by even a
little between the two occurrences defeats the match. When the optimizer can drop
an unused aggregate it will, and two sides that look different can collapse into
one that is reused, so read the plan rather than the query: look for a
\texttt{ReusedExchange} node, and count the scan stages.

\section{Unpersist and cache thrashing}
\label{sec:caching-unpersist}

A cached DataFrame you no longer need should be explicitly released with
\texttt{unpersist()}. Left alone, storage memory fills with DataFrames nobody
reads, and Spark's LRU eviction discards whichever cached \emph{block} was used
least recently to make room for new ones. Eviction happens per block, one per
partition, not per DataFrame, so a large cache is evicted piecemeal.

The lab shows it. With the unified pool shrunk to about 1.1~GB
(\conf{spark.memory.fraction}\,=\,0.3 on a 4~GB heap), three 8-day slices of
\texttt{orders}, 469~MB each at \texttt{MEMORY\_ONLY}, cannot all stay resident
(\Cref{fig:cache-thrash-lru}). After caching \texttt{ca}, \texttt{cb}, then
\texttt{cc}, \texttt{ca} had lost 4 of its 8 partitions, the least recently used
blocks, while \texttt{cb} and \texttt{cc} were whole. Re-reading \texttt{ca} then
took 8.5~s, because the missing half was recomputed from the source, and
re-caching it evicted half of \texttt{cb} in turn. Re-reading \texttt{cc}, still
fully cached, took 0.4~s.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{cache-thrash-lru.pdf}
  \caption{Three caches that do not fit together. Each new cache evicts the least
  recently used blocks of an older one, and re-reading the damaged cache evicts
  its neighbour: LRU has no notion of which DataFrame you still need.}
  \label{fig:cache-thrash-lru}
\end{figure}

LRU picks purely on recency of read, so it can evict the cache you still need
before its next read, forcing a recompute you cached specifically to avoid. This
\emph{cache thrashing} is a caching bug that looks like a memory bug: the fix is
fewer, more deliberate caches (and prompt \texttt{unpersist()} on ones you are
done with), not more memory.

\begin{note}
Storage memory protects a cache from being evicted, not the executor from running
out of heap. On the lab, caching a third 586~MB slice with a 4~GB heap and eight
concurrent scan tasks ended in \texttt{java.lang.OutOfMemoryError: Java heap
space}; the unified pool is soft, but the JVM heap is not.
\end{note}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:caching-summary}

Cache what is read enough times to repay the build and fits the storage budget;
on the lab that took about ten reads of a cheap source. \texttt{cache()} only
marks a DataFrame lazily, so materialize it on your own schedule with a cheap
action; \texttt{CACHE TABLE} in SQL is eager by contrast. A DataFrame cache is
columnar batches, so the serialized level saves about a quarter, not a multiple.
Execution and storage share unified memory and may borrow each other's idle
space, but only execution can evict a borrower to reclaim it: the lab saw 322~MB
of a cache move to disk when a join started. Spark reuses an identical exchange
on its own, fragile to any difference between the two occurrences; and a cache
you forget to release does not just waste memory, it can evict the cache you
still need. \Cref{tab:caching-levers} turns each symptom into the evidence and
the lever.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}P{3.3cm}P{3.9cm}P{3.7cm}P{3.1cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Symptom} & \textbf{Evidence} & \textbf{Lever} & \textbf{Cost} \\
\midrule
a cache made the job slower & build time above the saving; few reads & remove the cache; compute the break-even & none \\
a cached DataFrame is not resident & empty \textbf{Storage} tab after \texttt{cache()} & run a materializing action; \texttt{CACHE TABLE} is eager & the build cost, paid up front \\
heavy GC with a large cache & GC time at 5--10\% of task time & serialized or off-heap level & decoding, off-heap capacity \\
a re-read is slow though cached & partial \texttt{cached/total} in \textbf{Storage} & fewer caches; \texttt{unpersist()} old ones & recompute on a miss \\
a plan scans the same table twice & two scan stages, no \texttt{ReusedExchange} & make the two sides identical & none \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The caching and reuse levers: symptom, evidence, lever, and cost.}
\label{tab:caching-levers}
\end{table}

The rule the chapter repeats: \textbf{a cache is a bet on the number of reads, so
measure the break-even before placing it}. \Cref{ch:iceberg-spec} turns from
keeping data in memory to the table format that organizes it on storage.
