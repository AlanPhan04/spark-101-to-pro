\chapter{Partitioning, Bucketing, and Pruning}
\label{ch:pruning}

\begin{chapterintro}
The cheapest data to move is data you never read. The specimen asks about one
week of a month of \texttt{orders}; a plan that reads all 31 days and filters
afterwards has done four times the work, and no later stage can undo it. This
chapter covers the ways Spark skips data before it moves any: partition pruning
skips whole directories, dynamic partition pruning does it using values
discovered from the other side of a join, bucketing lays data out so a join
needs no shuffle, and column pruning reads only the columns the query names.
Each is easy to defeat --- a function on the partition column, a \texttt{CAST},
a stray \texttt{SELECT *} --- and the plan tells you when you have. Every plan
and byte count below was measured on the lab of \Cref{sec:refenv-lab}, and two of
them overturned what the first draft of this chapter assumed.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Reading a scan node}
\label{sec:pruning-scan}

Every pruning mechanism in this chapter reports itself in one place: the scan
node at the bottom of the plan. Learn its fields first, because the rest of the
chapter is about making them say the right thing. \Cref{tab:pruning-scan-fields}
lists them.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}P{3.1cm}P{5.4cm}P{5.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{What it says} & \textbf{An empty value means} \\
\midrule
\conf{PartitionFilters} & predicates resolved against partition values, so whole partitions are skipped & the filter did not resolve against the partition column; every partition is read \\
\texttt{PushedFilters} & predicates handed to the file reader, which skips row groups by their min/max statistics & nothing was pushed; rows are filtered after they are decoded \\
\texttt{ReadSchema} & the columns the scan actually reads & (never empty) a schema wider than the query needs is a pruning failure \\
\texttt{RuntimeFilters} & filters injected at run time, such as dynamic partition pruning's (\Cref{sec:pruning-dpp}) & no runtime filter was added \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The scan-node fields that report pruning. An empty list on a
partitioned table is the first thing to look for when a scan reads too much.}
\label{tab:pruning-scan-fields}
\end{table}

The names depend on the reader. A Hive-catalogued Parquet table, read through
Spark's built-in file source, prints \conf{PartitionFilters},
\texttt{PushedFilters}, and \texttt{ReadSchema} on a \texttt{FileScan} node. An
Iceberg table is read through the DataSource~V2 API, and its \texttt{BatchScan}
node prints the pushed predicates inside a single \texttt{filters=[\ldots]} list
instead, the columns it reads in square brackets after the table name, and
\texttt{RuntimeFilters} beside them. The meaning is the same; the spelling
differs.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=text,numbers=none,style=plan,caption={The specimen's Iceberg scan node (\texttt{EXPLAIN}, one line wrapped)},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-scan-node]
BatchScan lab.db.orders[order_ts, product_id, promised_days, delivery_days]
  lab.db.orders (branch=null)
  [filters=order_ts IS NOT NULL, order_ts >= 1772323200000000,
           order_ts < 1772928000000000, groupedBy=]
  RuntimeFilters: []
\end{lstlisting}

Read it field by field. The four columns in brackets are the \texttt{ReadSchema}
equivalent: the specimen names \texttt{order\_ts}, \texttt{product\_id},
\texttt{promised\_days}, and \texttt{delivery\_days}, and nothing else, so
\texttt{order\_id}, \texttt{customer\_id}, and \texttt{order\_total} are never
read. The two \texttt{order\_ts} comparisons are the week's bounds, already
converted to microseconds since the epoch; they are the predicates that decide
which partitions survive. \texttt{RuntimeFilters} is empty because nothing in
this query adds one.

\section{Partition pruning and the pushdown killers}
\label{sec:pruning-static}

A table physically partitioned by a column --- for a Hive-catalogued table, one
subdirectory per distinct value; for Iceberg, the hidden partitioning covered in
\Cref{ch:iceberg-layout} --- lets the planner skip whole partitions before any
file inside them is opened, whenever a filter can be resolved against the
partition value alone. \Cref{fig:pruning-static} shows the specimen's case:
\texttt{orders} is partitioned by day, the week's filter matches 7 of the 31
daily partitions, and the other 24 are discarded at plan time, without opening
a file or reading a footer.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pruning-static.pdf}
  \caption{A filter that resolves against the partition value keeps 7 of 31
  daily partitions of \texttt{orders} and skips the other 24 before any file is
  opened. Green marks what is read; dashed gray marks what is skipped.}
  \label{fig:pruning-static}
\end{figure}

Put numbers on it, from the lab. For the columns the specimen needs, one day of
\texttt{orders} is 6.7~MB of Parquet and the month is 207.3~MB, so the week reads
46.8~MB in 7 tasks and an unpruned scan reads the month in 31. That is 4.4 times
the bytes to answer the same question, and every extra byte becomes extra tasks
and extra shuffle input downstream.

The most common reason pruning does not happen is the filter itself. A filter
resolves against a partition value only when Spark can reduce it to a comparison
of the bare column with a literal. The first draft of this chapter listed
\texttt{date(order\_ts)} and \texttt{CAST(order\_ts AS DATE)} among the filters
that break this. The lab says otherwise. \Cref{tab:pruning-killers} gives five
ways to ask for one day, and what each scan did; \Cref{fig:scan-bytes-by-filter}
plots the bytes.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}P{5.3cm}P{3.6cm}rr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Filter as written} & \textbf{What the scan received} & \textbf{Input} & \textbf{Tasks} \\
\midrule
\texttt{order\_ts >= \ldots{} AND order\_ts < \ldots} & the range, as written & 6.7~MB & 1 \\
\texttt{date(order\_ts) = DATE '2026-03-03'} & rewritten to the same range & 6.7~MB & 1 \\
\texttt{CAST(order\_ts AS DATE) = DATE '\ldots'} & rewritten to the same range & 6.7~MB & 1 \\
\texttt{CAST(order\_ts AS STRING) LIKE '2026-03-03\%'} & no pushed filter & 207.3~MB & 31 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{One day of \texttt{orders}, five ways. Spark rewrites a date comparison
on a timestamp column into a range on the bare column, and the range prunes.
A string cast or arithmetic on the column leaves nothing to push, and the scan
reads the whole month.}
\label{tab:pruning-killers}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{scan-bytes-by-filter.pdf}
  \caption{The same one day of \texttt{orders}, five filters, measured. Three forms reach the scan as a range and read 6.7~MB in one task; the two that do not read the whole month, 31 times as many tasks and 31 times the bytes.}
  \label{fig:scan-bytes-by-filter}
\end{figure}

The pattern is that Spark does the rewriting for comparisons it recognizes as
equivalent to a range on the column, and gives up on anything it does not. So
the rule is not ``no function on the column''; it is ``read the scan node and see
what arrived.'' A filter that arrives as a range prunes. A filter that does not
appear in \texttt{filters=} at all is being evaluated after the data has been
read. The full catalogue of pushdown killers, with the fix for each, is
\Cref{sec:c-pushdown}. The rewriting happens in Spark's optimizer before the
scan, so it does not depend on the catalog, but check your own table's scan node
once rather than assuming.

The specimen's own filter works for the simplest reason: it already is a range on
the bare column, resolved once at plan time, with nothing standing between the
column and the value.

\section{Dynamic partition pruning}
\label{sec:pruning-dpp}

Static pruning needs a literal known before the query runs. A star-schema join
--- fact table filtered indirectly, through a small dimension --- has no literal
for the planner to use:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL,caption={A filter on the dimension, not the fact table},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-dpp-query]
SELECT COUNT(*) AS n, SUM(o.order_total) AS total
FROM orders_d o
JOIN dim_date d ON o.order_date = d.date
WHERE d.day_of_week = 'Sat' AND d.month = 3;
\end{lstlisting}

Here \texttt{orders\_d} is a copy of \texttt{orders} partitioned by an identity
transform on a \texttt{DATE} column, \texttt{order\_date}; the lab builds it with
a \texttt{CREATE TABLE \ldots{} PARTITIONED BY (order\_date) AS SELECT *,
CAST(order\_ts AS DATE) AS order\_date}. The next subsection explains why the
specimen's own \texttt{day(order\_ts)} layout is not used.

Without help, Spark scans every partition of \texttt{orders\_d}, joins, and
discards everything that is not a Saturday --- all the pruning happens
\emph{after} the expensive read. \emph{Dynamic partition pruning} (DPP, on by
default via \conf{spark.sql.optimizer.dynamicPartitionPruning.enabled}) rewrites
this (\Cref{fig:dpp-flow}). It runs the dimension side first, collects the
matching \texttt{date} values, and injects them into the fact scan as if the query
had been written with \texttt{WHERE o.order\_date IN (SELECT date FROM dim\_date
WHERE \ldots)}, so the same partition-level skipping from
\Cref{sec:pruning-static} applies to a filter that, in the query as written,
lived on the other table entirely.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{dpp-flow.pdf}
  \caption{Without DPP, pruning happens last: the whole fact table is scanned
  before the join discards most of it. With DPP, the small dimension side runs
  first and its matching keys are injected as a filter \emph{before} the fact
  scan, so pruning happens first.}
  \label{fig:dpp-flow}
\end{figure}

The lab measures the saving. March 2026 has four Saturdays, so DPP should keep 4
of 31 partitions. \Cref{tab:pruning-lab-dpp} shows what the scan did across the
cases that matter.

\subsection{When DPP applies}

The table is the chapter's best argument for reading \texttt{RuntimeFilters}
instead of trusting the plan's shape. The rows with a filter that prunes nothing
are the interesting ones, and the conditions in
\Cref{tab:pruning-dpp-conditions} explain each of them.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}P{4.3cm}P{5.0cm}P{4.7cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Why it is required} & \textbf{What fails without it} \\
\midrule
The fact table is partitioned by the join column itself & the injected filter is an \texttt{IN} on that column, and the scan must be able to map it onto partitions & the \texttt{day(order\_ts)} row: a filter is injected, 31 tasks still run \\
The dimension side can be reused as a broadcast & the keys come free from the broadcast that the join builds anyway (\conf{spark.sql.optimizer.dynamicPartitionPruning.reuseBroadcastOnly}, default \texttt{true}) & the broadcast-disabled row: the filter degrades to \texttt{true} \\
The dimension side has a selective filter & without one, every key matches and nothing is pruned & a \texttt{WHERE} on the dimension \\
The join is an equi-join on the partition column & the injected filter is an \texttt{IN} on that column & the join condition \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Conditions for dynamic partition pruning, each with the lab row that
shows it failing.}
\label{tab:pruning-dpp-conditions}
\end{table}

The broadcast condition deserves its reason. Spark runs the dimension side once,
builds the broadcast hash table for the join out of it as it would anyway, and
reuses that \emph{same} in-memory result a second time as the list of matching
keys for the fact-table filter: one execution, two consumers. In the lab's final
plan the scan's subquery node reads \texttt{ReusedExchange} over the join's own
\conf{BroadcastExchange}. If the dimension side is not broadcastable there is
no cheap, already-materialized copy to reuse, and with the default
\conf{reuseBroadcastOnly} the filter collapses to
\conf{dynamicpruningexpression(true)}, a sentinel that means ``no pruning.''
With \conf{reuseBroadcastOnly} set to \texttt{false} Spark may run the
dimension side as its own query, but only when its estimate says the pruning
saves more than it costs
(\conf{spark.sql.optimizer.dynamicPartitionPruning.fallbackFilterRatio}, default
0.5). Leave the default alone unless you have measured.

The first condition is the one a hidden-partitioned Iceberg table trips. The
specimen's \texttt{orders} is partitioned by \texttt{day(order\_ts)}, so no
column called \texttt{order\_date} exists to join on, and a join on
\texttt{CAST(order\_ts AS DATE)} got a runtime filter that the Iceberg scan could
not turn into a partition skip: 31 tasks, 207.3~MB, identical to the run with
DPP off. Whether a different Iceberg version or a different join expression
closes that gap is worth testing on your table; until you have, treat a DPP
filter on a transformed partition column as unproven.

\begin{tip}
\texttt{RuntimeFilters: [dynamicpruningexpression(true)]} is not a pruning filter.
It is Spark telling you DPP considered the join and decided not to act. When a
star join scans too much, look for that line before anything else.
\end{tip}

\section{Bucketing to avoid a shuffle}
\label{sec:pruning-bucketing}

Partitioning divides a table by directory; \emph{bucketing} divides it further,
within a partition, by hashing a column into a fixed number of files. Writing
with \texttt{bucket(16, customer\_id)} computes
\texttt{hash(customer\_id) mod 16} for every row and sends it to the file for
that remainder. This is the same \texttt{hash(key) mod N} idea
\Cref{sec:shuffle-write} used to assign shuffle output partitions, applied at
write time to decide which \emph{file} a row lands in instead of which in-flight
partition. Two tables bucketed on the same column with the same bucket count
assign every row the same way from the same \texttt{customer\_id}, so a row and
its join partner always land in the file pair with the same bucket number.

\Cref{fig:bucketed-join} shows what that buys. An ordinary join pays two
shuffles every time it runs; a bucketed join pays them once, at write time.
Each task reads one bucket from each table and joins them directly, which is
precisely the precondition \Cref{sec:joins-spj} used for a storage-partitioned
join.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{bucketed-join.pdf}
  \caption{Without bucketing, a join shuffles both sides to line up the keys.
  With both tables bucketed on the join key, the matching rows are already in the
  same-numbered bucket on each side, and each task joins one bucket pair with no
  \texttt{Exchange}.}
  \label{fig:bucketed-join}
\end{figure}

The lab bucketed \texttt{orders} and \texttt{dim\_customer} on
\texttt{bucket(16, customer\_id)} and joined them with broadcasting disabled.
Without Spark's storage-partitioned join enabled, the plan has two
\texttt{Exchange hashpartitioning(customer\_id, 200)} nodes under the join, and
the reduce stage read 226.9~MB of shuffle data across 45 tasks. With it enabled,
the scans carry \conf{groupedBy=customer_id_bucket}, the two exchanges are
gone, and the join runs in the 16 tasks of the scan stage itself with no shuffle
read. The settings the lab turned on were
\conf{spark.sql.sources.v2.bucketing.enabled},
\conf{spark.sql.iceberg.planning.preserve-data-grouping}, and
\conf{spark.sql.requireAllClusterKeysForCoPartition} set to \texttt{false}; the
lab did not test which of the three can be left at its default.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=text,numbers=none,style=plan,caption={A join over two bucketed Iceberg tables: no \texttt{Exchange} under the join (\texttt{EXPLAIN}, trimmed)},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-bucketed-plan]
+- SortMergeJoin [customer_id], [customer_id], Inner
   :- Sort [customer_id ASC NULLS FIRST]
   :  +- BatchScan lab.db.orders_b[customer_id]
   :       [filters=customer_id IS NOT NULL, groupedBy=customer_id_bucket]
   +- Sort [customer_id ASC NULLS FIRST]
      +- BatchScan lab.db.dim_customer_b[customer_id, segment]
           [filters=customer_id IS NOT NULL, groupedBy=customer_id_bucket]
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{Exchange hashpartitioning(segment, 200)} above the join is the final
aggregation's, and it appears in both plans; bucketing removes only the shuffle
that lined up the join keys.

\subsection{Sizing the bucket count}

The bucket count is a design decision with a hard constraint: it fixes the join's
parallelism and the size of each task's input, and you cannot change it without
rewriting the table. Size it the way \Cref{sec:shuffle-sizing} sizes shuffle
partitions. Suppose \texttt{orders} grows to 64~GB for the period you join on.
Sixteen buckets would put 4~GB in every task, which spills; at a 128~MB target
you need $64\,\text{GB} / 128\,\text{MB} = 512$ buckets. On 40 slots that is
about 13 waves, a comfortable number. Too many buckets is the opposite
failure: thousands of tiny files, and a task per file.

Bucketing has three costs you accept in exchange:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rigidity.} Both tables need the same transform and the same bucket
        count on the join column. A join on any other column gets no benefit.
  \item \textbf{No skew rescue.} A hot key still lands in one bucket. AQE's skew
        split (\Cref{sec:aqe-skew}) is a rewrite of a shuffle's output, and a
        bucketed join has no shuffle for it to act on, so a hot key becomes a
        slow task and stays one. The lab bucketed \texttt{orders} and \texttt{dim\_product}
into 16 buckets by \texttt{product\_id}, joined them with storage-partitioned join
on (no \texttt{Exchange} before the join), and read the join stage's 16 tasks: the
median took 3.3~s, the task holding the hot product's bucket 8.7~s --- 2.6 times the
median, and nothing in the plan could split it.
  \item \textbf{Write cost.} Every write into the table pays the hash and the
        fan-out to every bucket.
\end{itemize}

Classic Spark bucketing (\texttt{df.write.bucketBy(n, col)}, for a
Hive-catalogued table) established the idea but is brittle: the bucketing is a
Spark-only convention, recorded in table properties, and invisible to any other
engine reading the same files. Iceberg's \texttt{bucket(n, col)} partition
transform (\Cref{sec:ice-partitioning}) is the modern equivalent on this stack,
recorded in the table's own metadata so any engine reading the table sees and can
exploit the same layout.

\section{\texttt{repartition} versus \texttt{coalesce}}
\label{sec:repartition-coalesce}

Both change how many partitions a DataFrame has; they get there by opposite
mechanisms, and \Cref{tab:shape-narrow-wide} already told you which is which.
\texttt{repartition(n)} is wide: it shuffles, so it can raise \emph{or} lower the
partition count and redistributes rows evenly across the new count regardless of
how they were distributed before. \texttt{coalesce(n)} is narrow: it only lowers
the count, by merging existing partitions with no shuffle, which also means it
cannot fix uneven input. Coalescing four huge partitions and four empty ones down
to four still leaves four huge partitions; only a shuffle can redistribute the
rows themselves.

\Cref{tab:pruning-repartition} lists every way to ask for a partition count,
because the DataFrame methods and the SQL hints do not all behave alike. The last
column was measured on the lab for the first three rows.

The \texttt{REBALANCE} hint (Spark 3.2 and later) is the one to reach for before
a write: AQE measures the shuffle output, splits oversized partitions, and merges
tiny ones, so the files that come out are close to the advisory size without you
guessing a count. It is controlled by
\conf{spark.sql.adaptive.optimizeSkewsInRebalancePartitions.enabled} (default
\texttt{true}).

\texttt{coalesce} has a second cost beyond skew that surprises people. Because it
adds no shuffle, it does not start a new stage: the narrow chain before it is
folded into the same tasks, and \emph{those} tasks are the only ones that exist.
\Cref{tab:pruning-lab-coalesce} and \Cref{fig:repartition-vs-coalesce} show it on two
weeks of \texttt{orders}, read and written to a no-op sink.

\texttt{coalesce(10)} on a 560-split scan would run the scan and filter on 10
tasks of 56 splits each instead of 560 tasks spread over 40 slots: about 14 waves
become 56 split-times in series on 10 of 40 slots. \conf{repartition(10)} adds a
shuffle but keeps the scan wide. Neither is free; the choice is whether you would
rather pay in a shuffle or in a slow narrow stage.

\begin{note}
The \texttt{shuffle} flag in \texttt{coalesce(n, shuffle=true)} belongs to the RDD
API (\texttt{rdd.coalesce}). The DataFrame method \texttt{df.coalesce(n)} has no
such flag; the DataFrame equivalent of ``coalesce with a shuffle'' is
\conf{df.repartition(n)}.
\end{note}

\section{Column pruning and \texttt{ReadSchema}}
\label{sec:pruning-column}

Column pruning --- reading only the columns a query touches --- is one of the
optimizer's heuristic rules (\Cref{sec:catalyst-rules}) and shows up as the scan's
column list (\Cref{lst:pruning-scan-node}). A list wider than the query needs
almost always traces back to a \texttt{SELECT *} in a view, a CTE, or a UDF's
input that the optimizer cannot see through. It pays most on a columnar format,
where each column is stored contiguously and an unread column costs no I/O at all
(\Cref{sec:refenv-columnar}): the specimen reads 4 of the 7 \texttt{orders}
columns, and \texttt{order\_id} and \texttt{customer\_id}, high-cardinality
strings that compress poorly, are among the three it skips.

It goes one level deeper for a nested (struct) column. Parquet and Iceberg store
nested fields independently enough that a query touching only
\conf{event.location.city} out of a wide \texttt{event STRUCT<\ldots>} column can
prune to that one leaf field, without reading its siblings. This is
\emph{nested schema pruning}
(\conf{spark.sql.optimizer.nestedSchemaPruning.enabled}, default \texttt{true}).
It is visible in the scan's schema as the struct type narrowed to the accessed
fields, not dropped whole:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=text,numbers=none,style=plan,caption={The read schema before and after nested pruning (shape only)},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-readschema]
-- SELECT event.location.city FROM events
ReadSchema: struct<event:struct<location:struct<city:string>>>   -- pruned

-- SELECT my_udf(event) FROM events    (whole struct passed to a UDF)
ReadSchema: struct<event:struct<location:struct<city:string,
                    lat:double,lon:double>,user:struct<...>,...>>
\end{lstlisting}
% CAPTURE: the lab has no nested table yet; add one to lab/gen_data.py and capture both scans

Whether this fires depends on the query projecting the leaf field explicitly;
passing the whole struct into a UDF or a later transformation reintroduces the
full column, the same way a \texttt{SELECT *} does. (The lab does not yet have a
nested table, so \Cref{lst:pruning-readschema} is the expected shape, not a capture.)

\begin{tip}
To find which pruning layer failed on a slow scan, read the scan node in this
order: the filters first, because they are the cheapest to fix and the largest
saving; then the column list; then \texttt{RuntimeFilters}. A scan that reads too
much usually fails the first of the three that you check.
\end{tip}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:pruning-summary}

Static partition pruning skips whole directories when the filter reaches the scan
as a range on the bare column --- Spark rewrites date comparisons on a timestamp
into one, but not string casts or arithmetic --- and the scan node's
\texttt{filters=} list (\conf{PartitionFilters} on a Hive table) is where you
see it. Dynamic partition pruning gets the same skipping for a filter that lives
on the other side of a join, by running that side first and reusing its
broadcast; on the lab it cut a 31-task scan to 4, but only when the fact table
was partitioned by the join column itself, and the filters that did not prune
still printed as filters.

Bucketing extends pruning-by-layout into join cost: two
tables bucketed identically on the join key need no shuffle to join, at the
price of a fixed bucket count and no skew rescue.

\texttt{coalesce} merges partitions without a shuffle and therefore cannot fix
skew, and it narrows the whole stage before it; \texttt{repartition} can fix both,
at the cost of a shuffle. Column pruning reaches inside a struct, as long as the
query projects the leaf field it needs. \Cref{tab:pruning-levers} turns each
symptom into the evidence and the fix.

The rule the chapter repeats is that \textbf{the cheapest byte is the one the plan
proves it never reads}: every mechanism here is verified in the scan node, not
assumed from the query. \Cref{ch:caching} turns to the data you must read, and
to keeping what is expensive to recompute.
