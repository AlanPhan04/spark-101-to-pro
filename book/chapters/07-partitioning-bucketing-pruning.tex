\chapter{Partitioning, Bucketing, and Pruning}
\label{ch:pruning}

\begin{chapterintro}
The cheapest data to move is data you never read. The specimen asks about one
week of a month of \texttt{orders}; a plan that reads all 31 days and filters
afterwards has done four times the work, and no later stage can undo it. This
chapter covers the ways Spark skips data before it moves any: partition pruning
skips whole directories, dynamic partition pruning does it using values
discovered from the other side of a join, bucketing lays data out so a join
needs no shuffle, and column pruning reads only the columns the query names.
Each is easy to defeat --- a function on the partition column, a \texttt{CAST},
a stray \texttt{SELECT *} --- and the plan tells you when you have. Here is how
to read it.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Reading a scan node}
\label{sec:pruning-scan}

Every pruning mechanism in this chapter reports itself in one place: the scan
node at the bottom of the plan. Learn its fields first, because the rest of the
chapter is about making them say the right thing. \Cref{tab:pruning-scan-fields}
lists them.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.1cm}p{5.4cm}p{5.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{What it says} & \textbf{An empty value means} \\
\midrule
\texttt{PartitionFilters} & predicates resolved against partition values, so whole partitions are skipped & the filter did not resolve against the partition column; every partition is read \\
\texttt{PushedFilters} & predicates handed to the file reader, which skips row groups by their min/max statistics & nothing was pushed; rows are filtered after they are decoded \\
\texttt{ReadSchema} & the columns the scan actually reads & (never empty) a schema wider than the query needs is a pruning failure \\
\texttt{RuntimeFilters} & filters injected at run time, such as dynamic partition pruning's (\Cref{sec:pruning-dpp}) & no runtime filter was added \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The scan-node fields that report pruning. An empty list on a
partitioned table is the first thing to look for when a scan reads too much.}
\label{tab:pruning-scan-fields}
\end{table}

The names depend on the reader. A Hive-catalogued Parquet table, read through
Spark's built-in file source, prints \texttt{PartitionFilters},
\texttt{PushedFilters}, and \texttt{ReadSchema} on a \texttt{FileScan} node. An
Iceberg table is read through the DataSource~V2 API, and its \texttt{BatchScan}
node prints the pushed predicates inside a single \texttt{filters=[\ldots]} list
instead, with \texttt{RuntimeFilters} beside it. The meaning is the same; the
spelling differs. (Claim --- verify the exact \texttt{BatchScan} wording on the
reference cluster.)

\begin{lstlisting}[language=text,numbers=none,basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,breaklines=false,caption={The shape of the specimen's Iceberg scan node},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-scan-node]
BatchScan orders[order_ts, product_id, promised_days, delivery_days]
  (branch=null) [filters=order_ts >= 1772323200000000,
                         order_ts <  1772928000000000,
                 groupedBy=]
  RuntimeFilters: []
\end{lstlisting}
% CAPTURE: the BatchScan line from EXPLAIN FORMATTED of the specimen on the reference cluster

Read it field by field. The four columns in brackets are the \texttt{ReadSchema}
equivalent: the specimen names \texttt{order\_ts}, \texttt{product\_id},
\texttt{promised\_days}, and \texttt{delivery\_days}, and nothing else, so
\texttt{order\_id}, \texttt{customer\_id}, and \texttt{order\_total} are never
read. The two \texttt{order\_ts} comparisons are the week's bounds, already
converted to microseconds; they are the predicates that decide which partitions
survive.

\section{Partition pruning and the pushdown killers}
\label{sec:pruning-static}

A table physically partitioned by a column --- for a Hive-catalogued table, one
subdirectory per distinct value; for Iceberg, the hidden partitioning covered in
\Cref{ch:iceberg-layout} --- lets the planner skip whole partitions before any
file inside them is opened, whenever a filter can be resolved against the
partition value alone. \Cref{fig:pruning-static} shows the specimen's case:
\texttt{orders} is partitioned by day, the week's filter matches 7 of the 31
daily partitions, and the other 24 are discarded at plan time, without opening
a file or reading a footer.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pruning-static.pdf}
  \caption{A filter that resolves against the partition value keeps 7 of 31
  daily partitions of \texttt{orders} and skips the other 24 before any file is
  opened. Green marks what is read; dashed gray marks what is skipped.}
  \label{fig:pruning-static}
\end{figure}

Put numbers on it. If a day's partition holds 2~GB, the month is 62~GB, and the
week reads 14~GB: a filter that fails to prune reads 62~GB to answer the same
question, 4.4 times the bytes, and every extra byte becomes extra tasks and
extra shuffle input downstream. At 128~MB per input split
(\conf{spark.sql.files.maxPartitionBytes}), that is roughly 112 tasks
versus 496, on a cluster with 40 slots.

The most common reason pruning does not happen is the filter itself. A filter
resolves against a partition value only when Spark can compare the column to a
literal as it is stored. \Cref{tab:pruning-killers} lists the usual breakages;
the full catalogue, with the exact fix for each, is \Cref{sec:c-pushdown}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.6cm}p{4.2cm}p{5.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Filter as written} & \textbf{Why it may not prune} & \textbf{Rewrite} \\
\midrule
\texttt{WHERE date(order\_ts) = '2026-03-01'} & a function wraps the column & a range on the bare column: \texttt{order\_ts >= \ldots AND order\_ts < \ldots} \\
\texttt{WHERE CAST(order\_ts AS STRING) LIKE '2026-03\%'} & a cast changes the type being compared & compare the timestamp to timestamp literals \\
\texttt{WHERE order\_ts + INTERVAL 1 DAY > now()} & arithmetic on the column, plus a non-deterministic function & move the arithmetic to the literal side \\
\texttt{WHERE order\_ts >= a OR product\_id = b} & an \texttt{OR} branch that is not on the partition column & split into a \texttt{UNION ALL} of two prunable queries \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Filters that commonly defeat partition pruning. A plain comparison of
the bare column to a literal is the shape that always works.}
\label{tab:pruning-killers}
\end{table}

\begin{note}
Whether a given rewrite is \emph{necessary} on an Iceberg table is a question
for \Cref{ch:iceberg-read}: Iceberg translates some predicates on a source column
through its partition transform, so a form that fails against a Hive partition
column may still prune there. Do not assume either way; check the scan node.
(Claim --- verify each row of \Cref{tab:pruning-killers} on the reference
cluster.)
\end{note}

The specimen's own \texttt{order\_ts >= TIMESTAMP '\ldots'} filter works
precisely because it is the last row's opposite: a plain comparison against a
literal, resolved once at plan time, with nothing standing between the column
and the value.

\section{Dynamic partition pruning}
\label{sec:pruning-dpp}

Static pruning needs a literal known before the query runs. A star-schema join
--- fact table filtered indirectly, through a small dimension --- has no literal
for the planner to use:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL,caption={A filter on the dimension, not the fact table},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-dpp-query]
SELECT p.category, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders o
JOIN dim_date d ON CAST(o.order_ts AS DATE) = d.date
JOIN dim_product p ON o.product_id = p.product_id
WHERE d.day_of_week = 'Sat'
GROUP BY p.category;
\end{lstlisting}

Without help, Spark scans every partition of \texttt{orders}, joins, and discards
everything that is not a Saturday --- all the pruning happens \emph{after} the
expensive read. \emph{Dynamic partition pruning} (DPP, on by default via
\conf{spark.sql.optimizer.dynamicPartitionPruning.enabled}) rewrites this
(\Cref{fig:dpp-flow}). It runs the dimension side first, collects the matching
\texttt{date} values, and injects them into the fact scan as if the query had
been written

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL,numbers=none,caption={The query DPP effectively runs},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-dpp-rewrite]
WHERE CAST(o.order_ts AS DATE) IN (SELECT date FROM dim_date WHERE day_of_week = 'Sat')
\end{lstlisting}

so the same partition-level skipping from \Cref{sec:pruning-static} applies to a
filter that, in the query as written, lived on the other table entirely. March
2026 has four Saturdays, so the scan keeps 4 of 31 partitions: 13\% of the
bytes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{dpp-flow.pdf}
  \caption{Without DPP, pruning happens last: the whole fact table is scanned
  before the join discards most of it. With DPP, the small dimension side runs
  first and its matching keys are injected as a filter \emph{before} the fact
  scan, so pruning happens first.}
  \label{fig:dpp-flow}
\end{figure}

\subsection{When DPP applies}

DPP is a rewrite with conditions, and missing any one silently disables it.
\Cref{tab:pruning-dpp-conditions} lists them.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.3cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.7cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Why it is required} & \textbf{How to check} \\
\midrule
The fact table is partitioned by the join column & there is nothing to skip by directory otherwise & the table's partition spec \\
The dimension side has a selective filter & without one, every key matches and nothing is pruned & a \texttt{WHERE} on the dimension \\
The dimension side can be reused as a broadcast & the keys come free from the broadcast that the join builds anyway (\conf{spark.sql.optimizer.dynamicPartitionPruning.reuseBroadcastOnly}, default \texttt{true}) & \texttt{BroadcastHashJoin} in the plan \\
The join is an equi-join on the partition column & the injected filter is an \texttt{IN} on that column & the join condition \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Conditions for dynamic partition pruning. The broadcast-reuse condition
is the one that most often surprises.}
\label{tab:pruning-dpp-conditions}
\end{table}

The broadcast condition deserves its reason. Spark runs the dimension side once,
builds the broadcast hash table for the join out of it as it would anyway, and
reuses that \emph{same} in-memory result a second time as the list of matching
keys for the fact-table filter: one execution, two consumers. If the dimension
side is not broadcastable, there is no cheap, already-materialized copy to reuse.
Spark would have to run the dimension side as its own separate query only to get
the pruning values. With \texttt{reuseBroadcastOnly} set to \texttt{false} it
will do exactly that, but only when its estimate says the pruning saves more than
it costs (\conf{spark.sql.optimizer.dynamicPartitionPruning.fallbackFilterRatio},
default 0.5, and a statistics estimate that Iceberg tables often lack
(\Cref{sec:joins-forcing})). Leave the default alone unless you have measured.

When DPP fires, the fact scan carries the injected filter:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=text,numbers=none,basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,breaklines=false,caption={The shape of a scan with dynamic partition pruning},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-dpp-plan]
BatchScan orders[...]
  RuntimeFilters: [dynamicpruningexpression(CAST(order_ts AS DATE) IN dynamicpruning#42)]
  ...
  +- SubqueryBroadcast dynamicpruning#42
     +- BroadcastExchange
        +- Filter (day_of_week = Sat)
           +- BatchScan dim_date
\end{lstlisting}
% CAPTURE: EXPLAIN FORMATTED of lst:pruning-dpp-query; with AQE on, the subquery node may print as SubqueryAdaptiveBroadcast

\begin{warning}
DPP on an Iceberg table depends on the Iceberg scan advertising runtime
filtering, and on Iceberg projecting a join on \texttt{CAST(order\_ts AS DATE)}
onto its hidden \texttt{day(order\_ts)} transform. Both depend on the Iceberg
version in \Cref{app:refenv}. (Claim --- verify \texttt{RuntimeFilters} is
non-empty in \Cref{lst:pruning-dpp-plan} on the reference cluster before relying
on this; \Cref{ch:iceberg-read} covers the Iceberg side.)
\end{warning}

\section{Bucketing to avoid a shuffle}
\label{sec:pruning-bucketing}

Partitioning divides a table by directory; \emph{bucketing} divides it further,
within a partition, by hashing a column into a fixed number of files. Writing
with \texttt{bucket(16, customer\_id)} computes
\texttt{hash(customer\_id) mod 16} for every row and sends it to the file for
that remainder. This is the same \texttt{hash(key) mod N} idea
\Cref{sec:shuffle-write} used to assign shuffle output partitions, applied at
write time to decide which \emph{file} a row lands in instead of which in-flight
partition. Two tables bucketed on the same column with the same bucket count
assign every row the same way from the same \texttt{customer\_id}, so a row and
its join partner always land in the file pair with the same bucket number.

\Cref{fig:bucketed-join} shows what that buys. An ordinary join pays two
shuffles every time it runs; a bucketed join pays them once, at write time.
Each task reads one bucket from each table and joins them directly, which is
precisely the precondition \Cref{sec:joins-spj} used for a storage-partitioned
join.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{bucketed-join.pdf}
  \caption{Without bucketing, a join shuffles both sides to line up the keys.
  With both tables bucketed on the join key, the matching rows are already in the
  same-numbered bucket on each side, and each task joins one bucket pair with no
  \texttt{Exchange}.}
  \label{fig:bucketed-join}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Sizing the bucket count}

The bucket count is a design decision with a hard constraint: it fixes the join's
parallelism and the size of each task's input, and you cannot change it without
rewriting the table. Size it the way \Cref{sec:shuffle-sizing} sizes shuffle
partitions. Suppose \texttt{orders} grows to 64~GB for the period you join on.
Sixteen buckets would put 4~GB in every task, which spills; at a 128~MB target
you need $64\,\text{GB} / 128\,\text{MB} = 512$ buckets. On 40 slots that is
about 13 waves, a comfortable number. Too many buckets is the opposite
failure: thousands of tiny files, and a task per file.

Bucketing has three costs you accept in exchange:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rigidity.} Both tables need the same transform and the same bucket
        count on the join column. A join on any other column gets no benefit.
  \item \textbf{No skew rescue.} A hot key still lands in one bucket. AQE's skew
        split (\Cref{sec:aqe-skew}) is a rewrite of a shuffle's output, and a
        bucketed join has no shuffle for it to act on, so a hot key becomes a
        slow task and stays one. (Claim --- verify on the reference cluster.)
  \item \textbf{Write cost.} Every write into the table pays the hash and the
        fan-out to every bucket.
\end{itemize}

Classic Spark bucketing (\texttt{df.write.bucketBy(n, col)}, for a
Hive-catalogued table) established the idea but is brittle: the bucketing is a
Spark-only convention, recorded in table properties, and invisible to any other
engine reading the same files. Iceberg's \texttt{bucket(n, col)} partition
transform (\Cref{sec:ice-partitioning}) is the modern equivalent on this stack,
recorded in the table's own metadata so any engine reading the table sees and can
exploit the same layout. Spark still needs to be told to use it:
\conf{spark.sql.sources.v2.bucketing.enabled} is off by default, and depending on
the Iceberg version a second Iceberg setting is also required. (Claim --- verify
the exact keys in the Iceberg Spark documentation for the version in
\Cref{app:refenv}.)

\begin{lstlisting}[language=text,numbers=none,caption={The shape of a join over two bucketed Iceberg tables: no \texttt{Exchange} under the join},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-bucketed-plan]
SortMergeJoin [customer_id], [customer_id], Inner
:- Sort [customer_id ASC NULLS FIRST]
:  +- BatchScan orders[...]  groupedBy=[bucket(512, customer_id)]
+- Sort [customer_id ASC NULLS FIRST]
   +- BatchScan dim_customer[...]  groupedBy=[bucket(512, customer_id)]
\end{lstlisting}

\section{\texttt{repartition} versus \texttt{coalesce}}
\label{sec:repartition-coalesce}

Both change how many partitions a DataFrame has; they get there by opposite
mechanisms, and \Cref{tab:shape-narrow-wide} already told you which is which.
\texttt{repartition(n)} is wide: it shuffles, so it can raise \emph{or} lower the
partition count and redistributes rows evenly across the new count regardless of
how they were distributed before. \texttt{coalesce(n)} is narrow: it only lowers
the count, by merging existing partitions with no shuffle, which also means it
cannot fix uneven input. Coalescing four huge partitions and four empty ones down
to four still leaves four huge partitions; only a shuffle can redistribute the
rows themselves.

\Cref{tab:pruning-repartition} lists every way to ask for a partition count,
because the DataFrame methods and the SQL hints do not all behave alike.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.6cm}p{3.0cm}p{3.2cm}p{4.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Call or hint} & \textbf{Shuffles?} & \textbf{Distributes by} & \textbf{AQE can change it?} \\
\midrule
\texttt{repartition(n)} & yes & round-robin & no: the count is honored \\
\texttt{repartition(cols)} & yes & hash of columns & yes: coalesced like any shuffle \\
\texttt{repartition(n, cols)} & yes & hash of columns & no: the count is honored \\
\texttt{repartitionByRange(cols)} & yes, after a sampling job & range of columns & yes \\
\texttt{coalesce(n)} & no & merges neighbors & not applicable \\
\texttt{/*+ REBALANCE */} & yes & balances sizes & yes: splits skew, merges small \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The ways to set a partition count. Only \texttt{repartition(n)} and
\texttt{repartition(n, cols)} fix the count against AQE's coalescing. (Claim ---
verify the AQE column on 3.5.2.)}
\label{tab:pruning-repartition}
\end{table}

The \texttt{REBALANCE} hint (Spark 3.2 and later) is the one to reach for before
a write: AQE measures the shuffle output, splits oversized partitions, and merges
tiny ones, so the files that come out are close to the advisory size without you
guessing a count. It is controlled by
\conf{spark.sql.adaptive.optimizeSkewsInRebalancePartitions.enabled} (default
\texttt{true}).

\texttt{coalesce} has a second cost beyond skew that surprises people. Because it
adds no shuffle, it does not start a new stage: the narrow chain before it is
folded into the same tasks, and \emph{those} tasks are the only ones that exist.
If \texttt{orders} scans as 560 splits and you \texttt{coalesce(10)} just before a
write, the scan and filter run on 10 tasks, each carrying 56 splits, instead of
560 tasks spread over 40 slots. The scan stage that took 14 waves now takes 56
split-times in series, using 10 of 40 slots: roughly four times slower, to save
files. \texttt{repartition(10)} would add a shuffle but keep the scan wide.
Neither is free; the choice is whether you would rather pay in a shuffle or in a
slow narrow stage.

\begin{note}
The \texttt{shuffle} flag in \texttt{coalesce(n, shuffle=true)} belongs to the RDD
API (\texttt{rdd.coalesce}). The DataFrame method \texttt{df.coalesce(n)} has no
such flag; the DataFrame equivalent of ``coalesce with a shuffle'' is
\texttt{df.repartition(n)}.
\end{note}

\section{Column pruning and \texttt{ReadSchema}}
\label{sec:pruning-column}

Column pruning --- reading only the columns a query touches --- is one of the
optimizer's heuristic rules (\Cref{sec:catalyst-rules}) and shows up as the scan's
\texttt{ReadSchema} (\Cref{sec:c-scan}); a \texttt{ReadSchema} wider than the query
needs almost always traces back to a \texttt{SELECT *} in a view, a CTE, or a
UDF's input that the optimizer cannot see through. It pays most on a columnar
format, where each column is stored contiguously and an unread column costs no
I/O at all (\Cref{sec:refenv-columnar}): the specimen reads 4 of the 7
\texttt{orders} columns, and \texttt{order\_id} and \texttt{customer\_id}, high
cardinality strings that compress poorly, are among the three it skips.

It goes one level deeper for a nested (struct) column. Parquet and Iceberg store
nested fields independently enough that a query touching only
\texttt{event.location.city} out of a wide \texttt{event STRUCT<\ldots>} column can
prune to that one leaf field, without reading its siblings. This is
\emph{nested schema pruning}
(\conf{spark.sql.optimizer.nestedSchemaPruning.enabled}, default \texttt{true}).
It is visible in \texttt{ReadSchema} as the struct type narrowed to the
accessed fields, not dropped whole:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=text,numbers=none,basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily,breaklines=false,caption={\texttt{ReadSchema} before and after nested pruning},captionpos=b,label=lst:pruning-readschema]
-- query: SELECT event.location.city FROM events
ReadSchema: struct<event:struct<location:struct<city:string>>>    -- pruned

-- query: SELECT my_udf(event) FROM events      (whole struct passed to a UDF)
ReadSchema: struct<event:struct<location:struct<city:string,lat:double,lon:double>,user:struct<...>,...>>
\end{lstlisting}
% CAPTURE: ReadSchema from both queries against a real nested table on the reference cluster

Whether this fires depends on the query projecting the leaf field explicitly;
passing the whole struct into a UDF or a later transformation reintroduces the
full column, the same way a \texttt{SELECT *} does.

\begin{tip}
To find which pruning layer failed on a slow scan, read the scan node in this
order: \texttt{PartitionFilters} (or \texttt{filters=}) first, because it is the
cheapest to fix and the largest saving; then \texttt{ReadSchema}; then
\texttt{PushedFilters}. A scan that reads too much usually fails the first of the
three that you check.
\end{tip}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:pruning-summary}

Static partition pruning skips whole directories from a literal filter, and
fails when the filter wraps or converts the column; the scan node's
\texttt{PartitionFilters} (Iceberg's \texttt{filters=}) is where you see it.
Dynamic partition pruning gets the same skipping for a filter that lives on the
other side of a join, by running that side first and reusing its broadcast,
provided the fact table is partitioned by the join key and the dimension side is
selective and broadcastable. Bucketing extends pruning-by-layout into join cost:
two tables bucketed identically on the join key need no shuffle to join, at the
price of a fixed bucket count and no skew rescue.

\texttt{coalesce} merges
partitions without a shuffle and therefore cannot fix skew, and it narrows the
whole stage before it; \texttt{repartition} can fix both, at the cost of a
shuffle. Column pruning reaches inside a struct, as long as the query projects
the leaf field it needs. \Cref{tab:pruning-levers} turns each symptom into the
evidence and the fix.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.3cm}p{3.9cm}p{3.7cm}p{3.1cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Symptom} & \textbf{Evidence} & \textbf{Fix} & \textbf{Cost} \\
\midrule
a scan reads every partition & empty \texttt{PartitionFilters} or \texttt{filters=}; partition count in the SQL tab & rewrite the filter as a bare-column comparison with a literal & none \\
a star join scans the whole fact table & no \texttt{RuntimeFilters} / \texttt{dynamicpruningexpression} & partition the fact table by the join key; filter the dimension; keep it broadcastable & a layout decision \\
a repeated join shuffles both sides every run & two \texttt{Exchange} nodes under the join & bucket both tables on the key, same count & write cost; fixed bucket count \\
a write produces thousands of tiny files or a few huge ones & file sizes in the output & \texttt{REBALANCE} before the write & a shuffle \\
a stage got slower after \texttt{coalesce} & few tasks in the scan stage & \texttt{repartition(n)} instead & a shuffle \\
\texttt{ReadSchema} lists unneeded columns & wide \texttt{ReadSchema} on the scan & project columns explicitly; avoid \texttt{SELECT *} in views & none \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The pruning and layout levers: each symptom, the evidence in the plan,
the fix, and what it costs.}
\label{tab:pruning-levers}
\end{table}

The rule the chapter repeats is that \textbf{the cheapest byte is the one the plan
proves it never reads}: every mechanism here is verified in the scan node, not
assumed from the query. \Cref{ch:caching} turns to the data you must read, and
to keeping what is expensive to recompute.
