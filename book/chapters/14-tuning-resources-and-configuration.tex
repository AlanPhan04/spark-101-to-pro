\chapter{Tuning Resources and Configuration}
\label{ch:tuning}

\begin{chapterintro}
This chapter does not hand you a table of recommended settings. It derives an
executor shape from the memory map in \Cref{ch:execmem} and the shuffle in
\Cref{ch:shuffle}, and then checks each derivation against a measurement: twelve
executor shapes running the same stage, eight shuffle-partition counts, four
dynamic-allocation setups, and four layers of configuration. The numbers come
from the lab in \Cref{sec:refenv-lab} (one JVM on one machine), so read them as
shapes of curves and thresholds, not as production sizes. The chapter closes with
a symptom-to-lever table, and with the parts of the stack --- Kyuubi, the
Kubernetes shuffle story --- that the lab cannot exercise and are marked as such.
\end{chapterintro}

\section{Executor sizing from the mechanism}
\label{sec:executor-sizing}

Sizing an executor is choosing three numbers --- \conf{spark.executor.cores},
\conf{spark.executor.memory}, \conf{spark.executor.memoryOverhead} --- and each one
is bounded by a mechanism from an earlier chapter. The cleanest way to see the
bounds is to hold the work constant and change only the shape.

\paragraph{The experiment.}
One stage, twelve shapes. The stage hash-partitions the 30-million-row
\texttt{orders} table by \texttt{customer\_id} into 8 shuffle partitions and sorts
each partition by \texttt{order\_ts} for a window function; with AQE off, every
partition holds about 3.75 million rows. The lab runs Spark in local mode, so the
single JVM \emph{is} the executor: the heap is the executor heap and the core count
is the slot count. The grid is heap $\in \{1, 2, 4\}$~GB by cores $\in \{1, 2, 4,
8\}$. \Cref{fig:spill-heatmap} shows what happened.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{spill-heatmap.pdf}
  \caption{The same stage under twelve executor shapes. The spill pattern follows
  a diagonal: it appears whenever the execution memory per concurrent task falls
  below a threshold, whichever of heap or cores moved it there. Each cell is one
  run.}
  \label{fig:spill-heatmap}
\end{figure}

The diagonal is the memory model from \Cref{sec:mem-model} made visible. The
unified pool is $(\text{heap} - 300~\text{MiB}) \times 0.6$ (\conf{spark.memory.fraction}),
and the lab confirms the formula on all three heaps (\Cref{tab:tuning-pools}). That
pool is shared by the tasks running at the same moment, so each task's fair share is
the pool divided by the core count --- and that, not the heap on its own, is what a
sort or a hash table runs into.

\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrrr@{}}

\midrule

\bottomrule

\label{tab:tuning-pools}
\end{table}

\Cref{fig:spill-vs-share} replots the twelve runs against that share. Every shape
with 298~MB per task or less spilled; every shape with 456~MB or more did not. The
stage's largest tasks therefore need somewhere between 300 and 450~MB of execution
memory --- the figure that a sizing exercise has to find, and the reason the
heap alone is the wrong thing to tune.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{spill-vs-share.pdf}
  \caption{Spill against execution memory per task. The twelve runs separate cleanly
  at a single threshold, not by heap size and not by core count.}
  \label{fig:spill-vs-share}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What did the spilling cost?} Less than the cores bought.
\Cref{fig:cores-time-gc} shows wall-clock time falling from 55--57 seconds on one
core to 15.6--16.9 seconds on eight, almost independent of the heap, even though the
small heaps spilled 0.4--0.6~GB to disk along the way. The 2~GB heap at four cores
spilled and took 21.9~s; the 4~GB heap at four cores did not and took 20.3~s, an
8\% difference from one run each. On a laptop SSD with the operating system's page
cache absorbing the spill files, a spill is cheap; on a cluster with network-attached
local disks it may not be, and this lab cannot say. What does grow steadily with
cores is garbage-collection time (right panel: 0.16~s to 1.0--1.8~s summed over all
tasks), because more concurrent tasks hold more live rows in the same heap.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{cores-time-gc.pdf}
  \caption{More slots buy time; more slots in the same heap cost GC. The rings mark
  runs that spilled. The three heaps lie almost on top of each other in the left
  panel: for this stage, spilling to a fast disk cost less than the run-to-run noise
  one would expect between single runs.}
  \label{fig:cores-time-gc}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Cores per executor} therefore trades two things: tasks run concurrently
(time falls) against the share each gets of the pool (spill appears) and the heap
they pollute together (GC grows). On this stack's Kubernetes deployment a commonly
cited ceiling is about five cores per executor, attributed to contention in the GCS
connector's per-JVM connection pool; the lab has no GCS, so that explanation is a
claim --- verify on your cluster by comparing scan-stage time at five and eight
cores on the same data. The memory-side reason to stop early is the one measured
above.

\textbf{Executor memory} is sized backward from the largest per-task need. For the
specimen's reference cluster (4 cores per executor, \Cref{app:refenv}) the lab
stage's 300--450~MB per task means a unified pool of $4 \times (300\ \text{to}\ 450)
= 1.2\ \text{to}\ 1.8$~GB, hence a heap of $1.2/0.6 + 0.3 \approx 2.3$ to $3.3$~GB.
\conf{spark.executor.memory}\,=\,\texttt{4g} sits above that range with headroom for
the storage side of the unified pool (\Cref{ch:caching}) and is the defensible first
choice; it is a prediction for the \emph{reference} data, and the same arithmetic
with your own largest-task need replaces the 300--450 figure.

\textbf{Memory overhead} (\conf{spark.executor.memoryOverhead}, the greater of 10\%
of executor memory or 384~MB) covers everything outside the heap: Tungsten off-heap
pages if enabled, thread stacks, metaspace, and PySpark's worker processes. The
formula is a floor, not a measurement of \emph{your} workload. When a container is
killed with no Java exception anywhere, the overhead was the number that was too
small; \Cref{sec:failure-signatures} reproduces that failure on purpose.

\begin{tip}
On Kubernetes the pod's memory limit is executor memory plus overhead plus any
off-heap size. Exceeding it kills the pod from outside the JVM, so the Spark log
shows nothing --- the evidence is in the pod's status, not in the executor's.
\end{tip}

\section{Parallelism: partitions against slots}
\label{sec:tuning-parallelism}

\conf{spark.default.parallelism} governs RDD operations with no other hint
(defaulting to the total core count); for DataFrame and SQL workloads
\conf{spark.sql.shuffle.partitions} (\Cref{sec:shuffle-sizing},
\Cref{sec:aqe-coalesce}) is the number that matters. It is also a resource-sizing
lever, because it decides how many of the executors' task slots a reduce stage can
fill. \Cref{fig:partitions-vs-slots} sweeps it from 2 to 5000 on the lab's 8 slots,
with AQE off, for two stages with opposite bottlenecks.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{partitions-vs-slots.pdf}
  \caption{The same sweep on two stages. When the scan dominates (left) the
  shuffle partition count is nearly invisible until it reaches the thousands. When
  the shuffle stage does the work (right) too few partitions leave slots idle and
  too many pay a per-task cost: a hyperbola with a flat bottom.}
  \label{fig:partitions-vs-slots}
\end{figure}

On the right, 2 partitions leave six of eight slots idle and take 35.9~s; 8
partitions (one wave) take 10.3~s; the bottom of the curve is 8.8~s at 40, and
the stage slows again to 15.7~s at 5000 --- the same time as 4 partitions, for the
opposite reason. On the left the scan stage (about 32 input tasks) does most of
the work, so the shuffle partition count hardly matters until 1000 and 5000
partitions add scheduling cost. The practical rule is the one \Cref{ch:shuffle}
derived from bytes, now with its other boundary: a partition count at or above the
slot count, and small enough that per-task overhead stays negligible. AQE's
coalescing (\Cref{ch:aqe}) automates the upper boundary; nothing automates the lower
one if the input to the shuffle is genuinely small.

\section{Dynamic allocation}
\label{sec:dynamic-allocation}

A fixed executor count is either wasted (idle executors between jobs in a long-lived
session) or insufficient (a burst of work with no room to grow).
\conf{spark.dynamicAllocation.enabled} lets Spark request and release executors
against the cluster manager as work arrives and drains: it adds executors when the
scheduler's pending-task backlog persists past
\conf{spark.dynamicAllocation.schedulerBacklogTimeout} (1~s default), and removes an
executor once it has been idle past
\conf{spark.dynamicAllocation.executorIdleTimeout} (60~s default), bounded by
\conf{spark.dynamicAllocation.minExecutors} and \texttt{.maxExecutors}.

The one condition that makes this safe is the failure mode of
\Cref{sec:failure-signatures}: an executor holding shuffle files that a later stage
still needs cannot be released. Spark refuses to start otherwise. With
dynamic allocation on and nothing protecting shuffle data, the lab's application
died at start-up:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=text,style=plan,caption={Dynamic allocation without a plan for shuffle data (Spark 3.5.2, standalone cluster)},captionpos=b,label=lst:tuning-da-error]
org.apache.spark.SparkException: Dynamic allocation of executors requires
one of the following conditions: 1) enabling external shuffle service through
spark.shuffle.service.enabled. 2) enabling shuffle tracking through
spark.dynamicAllocation.shuffleTracking.enabled. 3) enabling shuffle blocks
decommission through spark.decommission.enabled and
spark.storage.decommission.shuffleBlocks.enabled. 4) (Experimental)
configuring spark.shuffle.sort.io.plugin.class ...
\end{lstlisting}

\Cref{fig:dynalloc-timeline} shows what shuffle tracking
(\conf{spark.dynamicAllocation.shuffleTracking.enabled}, on the lab cluster set
explicitly) does in practice. The lab ran a standalone master and one four-core
worker, executors of one core each, a maximum of four, and a 5-second idle timeout.
Each run executes a job, idles for 25~s, and then runs the \emph{same} DataFrame
again, so that a surviving shuffle can be reused. (A first version of this
experiment built a new DataFrame for the second job; Spark then recomputed the map
stage whatever happened to the executors, and the comparison measured nothing.)

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{dynalloc-timeline.pdf}
  \caption{Executors over time, three configurations. Left: job~1 leaves shuffle
  files, and tracking keeps all four executors through the idle period. Middle: the
  same, with a tracking timeout of 8~s; the executors holding the files are
  released and the second job has to ask for them again. Right: a job that leaves
  no shuffle files, so every executor is released after its idle timeout.}
  \label{fig:dynalloc-timeline}
\end{figure}

Tracking makes scale-down conservative: Spark keeps an executor while it holds
shuffle output that a live job or a re-run could use, and drops it only after
\conf{spark.dynamicAllocation.shuffleTracking.timeout} (infinite by default). The
price is visible in the left panel: four executors sit idle for 25~seconds because
their files might be needed again --- and the benefit is a second job that
finishes in 0.2~s because its map stage is skipped, compared to 25.6~s when the
executors are let go and the stage recomputed on freshly requested ones. An external shuffle service removes the
price by keeping the files servable without the executor; the lab ran no external
service, so that statement rests on the Spark documentation and the mechanism in
\Cref{sec:shuffle-external}, not on a measurement.

\section{The external and push-based shuffle service}
\label{sec:shuffle-external}

Without an external shuffle service, a reduce task fetches shuffle blocks directly
from the executor that wrote them (\Cref{sec:shuffle-write}) --- the dependency that
dynamic allocation has to work around, and the one that turns a premature executor
removal into a \texttt{FetchFailedException}. \conf{spark.shuffle.service.enabled}
moves block serving out of the executor JVM into a separate long-lived process, so
shuffle data stays servable after the executor that wrote it is gone and scale-down
carries no data-loss risk. Push-based shuffle (\Cref{sec:shuffle-push}) builds on
the same external service to merge blocks on the reduce side.

\begin{warning}
Claim --- from the Spark documentation, not tested in the lab: on YARN the external
shuffle service is a NodeManager auxiliary service; on Kubernetes there is no such
service, and dynamic allocation there relies on shuffle tracking (above) or on
decommissioning. Before relying on \conf{spark.shuffle.service.enabled} on a
Kubernetes cluster, confirm from the executor logs that a shuffle service endpoint
exists. Setting the key with nothing listening produces fetch failures that look
exactly like the problem it was meant to prevent.
\end{warning}

\section{Configuration precedence and profiles}
\label{sec:tuning-precedence}

A key can be set in more than one place, and Spark resolves a conflict by a fixed
order. The lab set the same key --- \conf{spark.sql.shuffle.partitions} --- in every
layer and asked the live session which value it saw (\Cref{tab:tuning-precedence}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{6.2cm}rr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Where the key was set} & \textbf{shuffle.partitions} & \textbf{executor.memory} \\
\midrule
properties file only (\texttt{spark-defaults.conf} equivalent) & 111 & 3g \\
file and \texttt{--conf} on \texttt{spark-submit} & 222 & 5g \\
file, \texttt{--conf} and \texttt{SparkSession.builder.config} & 300 & 5g \\
the above, then \texttt{spark.conf.set} on the running session & 400 & --- \\
\texttt{spark.conf.set("spark.executor.memory", ...)} on the running session & rejected & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The value a session reported after each layer was added. Each layer
overrides the one above it in the table; static settings such as executor memory
cannot be changed once the application has started.}
\label{tab:tuning-precedence}
\end{table}

The order is therefore: a runtime \texttt{SET} for SQL settings, then values set in
application code, then \texttt{--conf} flags, then \texttt{spark-defaults.conf},
then the built-in default. The code layer in the experiment set only the SQL key,
so the memory column carries the \texttt{--conf} value forward unchanged. The rejected last row is
the practical rule: only SQL configuration is changeable mid-session. A value that
looks ignored is usually overridden from a higher layer than the one you edited, and
\texttt{spark.conf.get(key)} in the running session reports the value actually in
effect --- faster than re-reading files.

\section{Kyuubi and multi-tenancy}
\label{sec:tuning-kyuubi}

\emph{Not exercised in the lab: there is no Kyuubi server here, so this section
describes the design rather than a measurement.} Kyuubi (this stack's gateway) runs
one Spark application per Kyuubi \emph{engine} rather than one per query, and its
\emph{share level} decides how engines are pooled and how a new connection finds its
engine. The server keeps a registry of live engines keyed by a scope derived from the
share level, backed by Zookeeper (or etcd) so it survives a server restart. A new
connection computes its key and either attaches to the engine registered under it (a
\emph{hit}) or launches a fresh Spark application and registers it (a \emph{miss}).
\Cref{tab:kyuubi-share-levels} lays out the three share levels this stack chooses
between.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.1cm}p{1.8cm}p{4.6cm}p{4.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Share level} & \textbf{Registry key} & \textbf{Isolation} & \textbf{Cold-start cost} \\
\midrule
\texttt{CONNECTION} & connection id & one engine per JDBC connection & every connection is a registry miss --- maximum startup latency, maximum isolation \\
\texttt{USER} & username & one warm engine per user, reused across their connections & only that user's first connection is a miss; every later one is a hit \\
\texttt{SERVER} & a constant & one engine shared by every user on the instance & almost always a hit, but every user's jobs compete for the same executors \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Kyuubi's share levels trade isolation for cold-start cost by changing what
the registry key is derived from.}
\label{tab:kyuubi-share-levels}
\end{table}

Under \texttt{USER} sharing, a user's first connection of the day pays the full cost
of launching a Spark application; her second connection an hour later computes the
same key, finds her running engine, and starts planning immediately. Under
\texttt{CONNECTION} sharing the second connection would compute a fresh key and pay
the cold start again --- the isolation costs exactly the warm reuse that
\texttt{USER} gives for free. The dynamic-allocation timeline above is the other half
of the picture: a warm engine is only cheap to keep if its executors scale down
between that user's queries. An \emph{engine pool} pre-registers several engines so
even a first connection is a hit. Each pooled engine is an ordinary Spark
application, sized as in this chapter's first section.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:tuning-summary}

An executor's three sizes each trace to a mechanism: the heap bounds the unified
pool as $(\text{heap} - 300~\text{MiB}) \times 0.6$; that pool divided by the cores is
the share each concurrent task spills against, and in the lab the threshold sat
between 298 and 456~MB per task; the overhead covers everything outside the heap.
Cores bought time (55~s to 16~s from one to eight) and cost GC; spilling to a fast
local disk cost far less than the cores returned. The shuffle partition count has a
lower boundary set by the slot count and an upper one set by task overhead, with a
flat bottom in between. Dynamic allocation is safe only when something protects
shuffle data; tracking does so by keeping executors, and the lab measured what
keeping them saves (0.2~s against 25.6~s) and what it holds idle. Configuration
resolves in a fixed order in which only SQL keys can still change at runtime.
Kyuubi's engine share level and the Kubernetes shuffle story are design points the
lab could not measure.

\exercisehead
\begin{exercises}
\item Pick a stage of your own that spills. From its task-level metrics
      (\Cref{sec:reading-metrics}) read the largest task's memory use, compute the
      unified pool per concurrent task, and predict whether halving the cores or
      doubling the heap removes the spill. Then test the prediction.
\item Repeat the shuffle-partition sweep of \Cref{fig:partitions-vs-slots} on a
      stage of your own with AQE off. Where is the bottom of your curve relative to
      the slot count?
\item Run a job, idle for longer than \conf{spark.dynamicAllocation.executorIdleTimeout},
      and run a second job that reuses the first job's shuffle. Compare the second
      job's duration with shuffle tracking on, with a short tracking timeout, and
      (on a cluster that has one) with an external shuffle service.
\end{exercises}
