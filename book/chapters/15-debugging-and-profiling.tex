\chapter{Debugging and Profiling}
\label{ch:debugging}

\begin{chapterintro}
A slow job leaves evidence: in the SQL tab, the stages tab, the executor
metrics, the event log, and --- when you need it --- a flame graph. This chapter
teaches you to read that evidence in order, so that "the job is slow" becomes
"stage 4 is skewed" or "the executors are GC-bound" in a few minutes. It also
catalogs the failure signatures --- OOM and container kills, fetch failures,
stragglers --- and what each looks like before you reach for a fix.
\end{chapterintro}

\paragraph{Fetch failures and stage retries.}

Shuffle files are not deleted as soon as they are read --- Spark keeps them in
case a later stage needs the same shuffle again, and cleans them up in the
background as the driver's JVM garbage-collects the objects that reference them
(the \emph{ContextCleaner}). In practice the deciding moment is simpler: that
storage is local to the executor that wrote it (\Cref{sec:shuffle-write}), and
on the reference stack it is an \texttt{emptyDir} tied to the executor's pod, so
losing the pod --- an OOM kill, a node drain, dynamic allocation scaling down
--- deletes its shuffle files immediately, with no way to recover them from that
executor again.

A reduce task that tries to fetch from such an executor gets a
\texttt{FetchFailedException}, visible in the driver log and the stage's tasks
tab. This is not a generic task failure: the DAG scheduler recognizes it
specifically, marks that executor's map output as gone in the map output
tracker, and resubmits only the map tasks that ran on it --- not the whole
stage, and not the job --- before retrying the reduce stage. You will see this
in the UI as a stage that appears to run twice, with a smaller second attempt.

\begin{tip}
Two different layers retry, and tuning the wrong one does nothing. A fetch that
fails from a flaky network but a still-live executor is retried by the shuffle
client itself: \conf{spark.shuffle.io.maxRetries} (3 attempts) and
\conf{spark.shuffle.io.retryWait} (5s between them). A fetch that fails because
the executor is genuinely gone is handled by the stage resubmission above,
bounded by \conf{spark.stage.maxConsecutiveAttempts} (4) and, per task,
\conf{spark.task.maxFailures} (4) --- raise both on a cluster where executors
are routinely reclaimed (spot instances, aggressive dynamic allocation) rather
than let the job fail after only a handful of retries. If the lost executors
are \emph{voluntary} removals under dynamic allocation
(\Cref{sec:dynamic-allocation}) rather than crashes,
\conf{spark.shuffle.service.enabled} avoids the failure entirely by keeping
shuffle data servable after its executor is gone (\Cref{sec:shuffle-push}).
Enabling \conf{spark.excludeOnFailure.enabled} keeps subsequent retries from
landing back on a node that is already causing them.
\end{tip}
